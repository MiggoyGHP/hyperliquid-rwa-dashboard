Stocks on a crypto exchange pay rent.
This page measures it.

Hyperliquid lists 24/7 futures on real stocks — Tesla, Nvidia, Apple and ~55 more. Every hour, one side of each market pays the other a small fee called the funding rate. When the rate is positive, people betting against the stock collect it. This dashboard tracks those payments live, compares them with the real stock and its options, and works out what a hedged "collect-the-rent" trade earns — and risks.

Step 1 · How the trade works

Collect the fee, cancel the price risk

The income leg — sell

Short the stock-perp on Hyperliquid

You "short" (bet against) the tokenized stock. While funding is positive you collect the hourly fee. Problem: if the stock rises, this leg loses money.

The hedge leg — buy

Own the same stock some other way

Buy the real stock, a deep in-the-money call option, or a "synthetic long" (buy a call, sell a put). If the stock rises, this leg gains what the short loses — and vice versa.

The result

Price moves cancel. The fee stays.

What's left is the funding you collected, minus costs. It's not free money: funding can flip negative, and the two legs live on different venues. The risks section below is the fine print.

      Methodology

      Funding: Hyperliquid public API, hourly rates, fetched live by your browser. "Annualized" multiplies an hourly rate by 24 × 365. Trailing windows sum the actual hourly rates in that window. Stock candles: Yahoo Finance daily data. Options: Cboe delayed quotes including delta (dividends ignored where we compute delta ourselves, which slightly flatters calls on dividend payers). Stock and options data refresh once per US trading day; funding is always live. Option contract counts are shown pro-rata (fractional); real trades round to whole 100-share contracts.

      What this is not

      Not financial advice, not an offer, and not a promise that any past funding rate continues. Funding can flip negative at any hour. The strategy shown involves leverage, options assignment risk, and venues that can fail independently of each other. Numbers here are estimates that exclude slippage, borrow costs, dividends and taxes.
